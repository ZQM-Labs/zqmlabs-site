AI systems can infer clues about places from buildings, vegetation,
language, road signs and skylines in pictures. That can be interesting,
but results can be wrong and an ordinary photo may reveal more about
a person than its owner intended.

## A privacy-first way to explore the idea

Use an **illustration of a fictional location**, or a specifically
licensed public landscape with no identifiable private subjects.
Ask what evidence supports each suggested location and what alternative
explanations fit the picture. A convincing answer is not necessarily a
verified answer.

Do not upload another person's private photo, school image, home view,
unpublished travel image, or camera-roll archive to locate someone.
Do not expose GPS EXIF metadata, addresses, or travel histories in
social scorecards.

For background, read [Bellingcat's October 2026 review](https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2026/10/22/can-llms-supercharge-geolocations-our-tests-show-big-improvements-but-humans-remain-key/),
which discusses improvements and failure modes in AI geolocation.
That article does not imply ZQM Labs has integrated an AI geolocator.

## From location guesses to a real group activity

Instead of trying to identify someone's private location, an
organizer can build original six-stop observation puzzles around
lawful, accessible public or permissioned venues. Teams share one
phone, follow a story, and solve clues together rather than exposing
their live coordinates.

Learn about [ZQM Labs games](/games/) and the
[quest authoring specification](https://github.com/ZQM-Labs/quest-authoring).
For a potential civic, visitor, or educational pilot, contact
[the Lab](mailto:zqmcomputing@gmail.com). Custom authoring and
availability require a separate scope review; neither AI photo
geolocation nor a ready-made city route is currently promised.
