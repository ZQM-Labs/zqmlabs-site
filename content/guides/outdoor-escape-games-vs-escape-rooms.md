## Two formats, one idea

Both formats give a team a story, a set of puzzles, and a clock. The difference is where the puzzles live — and what that does to the group.

### Indoor escape room

A team is locked — or, more often these days, simply placed — in a themed room. Find the clues, solve the puzzles, get out (or finish the story) before the time runs out. One room, one team, one hour.

- **Group size:** usually two to eight players per room. Larger groups split across several rooms and never play together.
- **Venue:** the operator's rooms. You go to them.
- **Weather:** irrelevant. Rain, heat, and storms do not exist inside.
- **Setup:** none for the players — book, show up, play.
- **Cost:** usually priced per person.

### Outdoor escape game

Escape-room puzzles taken outside: a story and a set of puzzles spread across a park or a neighborhood, usually timed, solved by one team working together on foot.

- **Group size:** one small team at a time — the puzzles are built for a group crowded around one problem.
- **Venue:** a park or neighborhood the operator uses.
- **Weather:** the whole game. Sun, rain, and heat are part of the day.
- **Setup:** none for the players; the operator sets the puzzles.
- **Cost:** usually per team or per person.

### The group-sized outdoor option: a location quest

There is a third shape worth knowing about, because it fixes the group-size problem both formats share. A location quest is a story-driven scavenger hunt: a team follows a trail of six stops, solves a puzzle at each one, and finishes together — and many teams can play the same route in parallel. One phone per team, browser-based, no app to install. Four comfort levels (Cozy, Spooky, After Dark, Overdrive) let the same quest fit children and adults. The organizer kit is designed to run in about an hour of setup, after the route and permissions are agreed with the Lab.

## When an indoor escape room genuinely fits better

Say it plainly: sometimes the room wins.

- **Bad weather with a fixed date.** If the date cannot move and the forecast is bad, a room is the safe choice. An outdoor game with no flexibility is a gamble.
- **A small team that wants a contained puzzle.** Two to six people who love puzzles and want to be locked in a room together — that is exactly what an escape room is for.
- **Mobility that suits one room.** A player who cannot manage outdoor terrain may be far more comfortable in a single accessible room. Check the venue's accessibility either way — never assume.
- **Late night.** An outdoor game after dark needs lighting, safety planning, and a venue that allows it. A room is lit, staffed, and open.

## When outdoor fits better

- **A whole group, not one team.** More than eight people, and an escape room splits the group across rooms that never meet. A location quest keeps every team on the same route, finishing at the same place.
- **Fresh air was the point.** If the team chose "outdoor" deliberately, a room does not deliver it.
- **Families with children.** Four comfort levels mean children and adults play the same quest. An escape room's puzzles and themes are usually built for adults.
- **Budget at group size.** Per-person pricing grows with the headcount. A flat fee — Family Night $49, Community $199, Office Team $349, Office Event $499 — does not.

## Weather plans for outdoor games

Florida weather is a planning input, not a surprise. A multi-day event window lets teams pick a dry moment. Morning or late-afternoon starts beat midday heat. Shade and water at the finish. And if a real storm wipes out the weekend, ask the Lab to extend the window — extensions are a conversation, not a guarantee, so ask before the event, not after.
