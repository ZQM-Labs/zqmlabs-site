A good city history scavenger hunt is a short, safe story told through
places—not a race, an unsourced historical fact sheet, or a list of random
GPS pins. Visitors and neighbors can enjoy a shared experience, but each
physical route needs permission, local knowledge, and testing.

## A six-stop planning pattern

1. **Welcome:** establish the theme and a safe orientation point.
2. **Origins:** use a verified historical record or interpretive display.
3. **Change over time:** compare two correctly dated and licensed sources.
4. **Creativity:** consider public art with artist attribution and permission.
5. **Community stories:** use consented quotations and inclusive context.
6. **Finale:** finish at a lawful, accessible gathering point with a recap.

These are **editorial suggestions**, not published ZQM Labs routes in a
specific city. Avoid residential sites, unauthorized QR placement,
sensitive locations, or making a puzzle depend on entering private land.

## Choose the right host model

- **DIY:** use an approved six-stop format, write and verify the puzzle
  content, arrange your own venue and access permissions, and test every
  stop with the intended audience.
- **Facilitated pilot:** ask about designing an event for a museum,
  downtown partnership, visitor organization, library or neighborhood
  group. Any custom story, branding, licensing and pricing require
  separate feasibility review and written agreement.
- **Current playable demo:** use the [Games directory](/games/) to find
  verified demo availability. A playable browser demo alone does not
  authorize installing signs or selling a new city route.

## Before promoting your event

Confirm your theme and audience, six lawful stops, weather and safe
crossings, sign permissions, mobility/sensory/reading alternatives, source
rights and a test run on real devices. A location-based game is only
ready for an event after its venue and software have been reviewed.
